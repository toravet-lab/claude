## CHAPTER 1: WHY SAYING NO FEELS SO RISKY

Most people won't say no at work because they're afraid.

Not of the person asking. Of what might happen after.

They imagine:

* Getting passed over for promotion
* Being labeled "difficult"
* Damaging the relationship
* Looking like they don't care
* Being seen as less committed than their peers

These fears are real. But they're usually not based on reality.

### The Fear That Keeps You Trapped

The biggest fear is this: **If I say no, my career will suffer.**

But here's what actually happens when you say no:

Your manager recalibrates what's realistic. Your peers adjust their expectations. People stop treating you like a dumping ground for low-value work.

The people who suffer aren't the ones who say no.

They're the ones who keep saying yes.

### The "Helpful Employee" Trap

You probably got here because you were reliable.

Early in your career, you said yes to things. You stayed late. You picked up projects nobody else wanted. And it worked. People noticed. You got promoted.

But there was a hidden cost.

Once you proved you'd say yes to anything, you became the default yes.

Every time someone needed something done, they thought of you. Not because you were the only option—but because they knew you wouldn't push back.

You'd been trained, basically. Rewarded for being available. And now you're stuck in a pattern where every "yes" creates the expectation for more yeses.

The people getting ahead aren't the ones doing the most work.

They're the ones doing the right work.

And that requires saying no.

### What "No" Actually Signals

Here's what people think happens when you say no:

*You're not a team player.*

Here's what actually happens:

*You're someone who prioritizes outcomes.*

When you say no to a low-value meeting, you're not saying "I don't care."

You're saying "I care enough about what I'm working on that I'm protecting time for it."

That's what high-performing people do.

They don't fill their time with whatever's urgent. They protect their time for what's important.

And that distinction—between urgent and important—is what separates people who burn out from people who thrive.

### The Cost of Saying Yes

Let's be specific about what happens when you say yes to something you shouldn't:

**Immediate costs:**
* Your current work gets delayed

**Medium-term costs:**
* You miss deadlines on your real priorities

**Long-term costs:**

* You miss growth opportunities because you're too busy
* You become less valuable, not more

People don't get fired for saying no thoughtfully.

## CHAPTER 2: WHAT "NO" ACTUALLY MEANS IN A PROFESSIONAL SETTING

The word "no" terrifies people.

But that's because they think "no" means rejection.

It doesn't.

**"No" is just prioritization.**

When you say no to something, you're not saying it's bad. You're saying it's not the right priority right now.

### Reframing the Conversation

Most people approach boundary-setting backwards.

They think: *How do I say no without being rude?*

The better question is: *How do I communicate my actual capacity and priorities?*

That shift changes everything.

Instead of "no" feeling like rejection, it feels like clarity.

Clarity about:
* What you can realistically do
* What's most important
* What trade-offs exist
* How decisions actually get made

Your boss doesn't want you saying yes to everything either.

What they want is honesty about what's possible.

If you say yes to something you can't actually deliver, that's worse than saying no upfront.

### The 4 Types of "No"

Not every situation calls for the same response.

Saying no to a meeting that you're not needed for is different from saying no to your boss about a deadline.

Here are the four main frameworks:

**1. Direct No**
Use this when: You can't do it, period.
Example: "I'm not able to take that on right now."
When it works: Low-stakes situations, clear capacity issues, trusted relationships.

**2. Conditional No**
Use this when: You could do it, but only if something changes.
Example: "I can take this on if we push deadline X, or add a resource."
When it works: Projects with flexibility, decisions involving trade-offs.

**3. Redirect No**
Use this when: It's not that you can't do it—someone else is better.
Example: "Sarah would be a better fit for this—she has more context on that system."
When it works: Distributing work fairly, building team capability, clear alternatives.

**4. Delayed No**
Use this when: Not now, but maybe later.
Example: "I can't commit to this right now, but I can revisit it in Q3 when capacity opens up."
When it works: Genuine future availability, high-priority work, good relationships.

Each of these is useful in different situations.

And once you know which one to use, the conversation becomes simple.

### The Power of Clarity Over Niceness

Here's what most people get wrong about boundaries:

They think the goal is to be liked.

The goal is to be trusted.

And you're trusted when you're honest about your capacity.

If you say yes to something you can't deliver, people stop trusting you. Your word becomes meaningless. That's when your reputation actually suffers.

But if you say no clearly, and then deliver on what you do commit to?

People trust you. They know your yes means something.

That's when your reputation actually grows.

### What Makes a No Actually Land

Three things:

**1. Calmness**
Don't apologize profusely. Don't over-explain. Calmness signals that you've thought about this and it's not personal.

**2. Clarity**
Be specific about why. Not emotional, just factual. "I'm at capacity" is clearer than "I have a lot going on."

**3. Consistency**
If you say no to one thing but yes to something similar, people will challenge it. Pick a standard and stick to it.

These three things—together—make boundaries feel professional, not defensive.

## CHAPTER 3: THE HIDDEN PLACES YOU NEED BOUNDARIES

Boundaries aren't just about saying no to big projects.

The biggest drain on your time usually comes from small, invisible places.

### Meetings That Shouldn't Exist

This is the biggest one.

Most meetings are not necessary.

Some statistics: workers spend 21+ hours a week in meetings. Most of that is wasteful. People join meetings out of habit, fear of missing something, or because they were added by default.

The pattern:
* Someone calls a meeting
* They add "just in case" people
* Those people show up
* Half the meeting doesn't apply to them
* Everyone's time gets wasted

You need a boundary here: **If I'm not needed, I shouldn't be there.**

That's not rude. That's efficient.

### "Quick Asks" That Aren't Quick

These are killing your productivity.

Someone Slacks you: "Quick question—do you have 5 minutes?"

Five minutes becomes 30. You lose focus. You have to context-switch back to what you were doing.

One "quick ask" per day might be fine.

But most people get 5-10 of these.

The boundary: async communication. Written out. With context. So you can respond on your schedule.

### Scope Creep Disguised as Collaboration

This is sneaky.

You agree to a project. The scope is defined. And then:

"While you're at it, could you also...?"

"Oh, we should probably add this too."

"Can you include that feature?"

Each add seems small. But they compound.

Suddenly you're doing 3x the original work.

The boundary: **Define done upfront. Question scope changes. Ask what gets deprioritized.**

### Slack/Teams Urgency Culture

Not everything that shows up in Slack is actually urgent.

But the notification makes it feel urgent.

People expect instant responses. They get frustrated if you don't reply immediately. The culture becomes: constant interruption is normal.

The boundary: **Not everything deserves an immediate response. Set expectations about your response time.**

### Unspoken Expectations

These are the worst.

Nobody explicitly says: "You should be available all weekend."

But the culture communicates it. People email you at 9 PM and expect responses. Your boss sends Slack messages at 6 AM.

The assumption becomes: *I should always be available.*

The boundary: **Work has hours. You have hours. That's a reasonable expectation.**

## CHAPTER 4: THE SCRIPT SYSTEM (CORE VALUE)

This is where theory becomes practical.

Below are real scripts you can use—right now—in the most common situations.

**How to use these:**
* Pick the script that fits your situation
* Adapt it to your language (use these as templates, not gospel)
* Practice saying it out loud first
* Notice that it doesn't feel as risky as you thought

### WORKLOAD PUSHBACK SCRIPTS

**When: Someone's asking you to take on more work and you're already at capacity.**

**Script 1 (Direct and honest)**
"I'm at capacity right now. If you want me to take this on, what should I deprioritize?"

*Why it works:* Shifts the responsibility back to them. Forces them to think about trade-offs.

**Script 2 (Positioning it as help)**
"I want to make sure I do this well—what's the timeline? And if it's urgent, what on my current list should move?"

*Why it works:* Shows you care about quality. Frames it as a logistics problem, not a refusal.

**Script 3 (Question first)**
"Help me understand the priority here—where does this fall relative to X and Y?"

*Why it works:* Doesn't say no directly. Just asks for clarity. Often they'll realize it's not that important.

**Script 4 (Offering a real solution)**
"I can't take this on this week, but I have bandwidth starting next week. Does that work?"

*Why it works:* You're not refusing—you're offering a timeline that actually makes sense.

**Script 5 (If it's your boss)**
"I want to make sure I'm focused on the highest-impact work. Can we go through my list and you tell me what takes priority?"

*Why it works:* Puts the decision on them. Shows you're committed to priorities, not just busy.

### DEADLINE NEGOTIATION SCRIPTS

**When: Someone wants something by a deadline that's not realistic.**

**Script 1 (State the reality)**
"Given everything on my plate, the earliest I can have this ready is [date]."

*Why it works:* No apologizing. Just a fact about your capacity.

**Script 2 (Offer a choice)**
"I can get you a rough draft by Friday, or a polished version by Monday. Which helps more?"

*Why it works:* You're not refusing—you're offering options and letting them choose.

**Script 3 (Link quality to timeline)**
"I want to make sure this is done right. Rushing this would mean cutting corners on X. Is that okay?"

*Why it works:* Forces them to think about what speed actually costs.

**Script 4 (Question the urgency)**
"What's driving the urgency? Is it truly needed by [date], or does it just feel urgent?"

*Why it works:* Sometimes people realize the deadline was arbitrary. Gives them room to adjust.

**Script 5 (Conditional yes)**
"I can have it by Wednesday if you can get me the final requirements by EOD Monday."

*Why it works:* You're saying yes, but with a clear condition that depends on them.

### SCOPE CREEP SCRIPTS

**When: The project keeps expanding and you never agreed to that.**

**Script 1 (Call it out directly)**
"That sounds like an expansion of the original scope. Should we revisit the timeline?"

*Why it works:* Neutral, factual. Not accusing. Just naming what's happening.

**Script 2 (Phase it)**
"Happy to include that—should we treat it as a phase two, or fold it into the original plan?"

*Why it works:* You're not refusing the work. You're asking how it fits.

**Script 3 (Ask what changes)**
"Adding that will push out the deadline. Should we extend the timeline, reduce scope elsewhere, or bring in another resource?"

*Why it works:* Makes trade-offs explicit. Puts the decision on them.

**Script 4 (Define done first)**
"Before we add more, can we define what 'done' looks like for the original scope?"

*Why it works:* Prevents scope from expanding by establishing a clear finish line.

**Script 5 (Redirect to decision-maker)**
"That's a good idea—should we flag it for [manager/stakeholder] to decide if it fits the original goals?"

*Why it works:* Removes you from the decision. Makes it about priorities, not you refusing.

### MEETING BOUNDARY SCRIPTS

**When: You're invited to a meeting you don't need to be in.**

**Script 1 (Direct and helpful)**
"I don't think I'm needed for this one—can you send me the notes if anything affects my work?"

*Why it works:* Helpful framing. Shows you care about outcomes, not just being present.

**Script 2 (Offer an alternative)**
"I probably don't have much to contribute here, but feel free to DM me with questions."

*Why it works:* You're accessible without committing the time.

**Script 3 (Question your presence)**
"I want to make sure I'm not wasting time—am I actually needed here?"

*Why it works:* Puts it back on them to justify your presence.

**Script 4 (Async option)**
"Could this be handled async? I could read a summary instead."

*Why it works:* Offers a real alternative that gets you information without the time sink.

**Script 5 (If it's recurring)**
"I've been on this recurring meeting for a while. Should I still be attending?"

*Why it works:* Good for meetings that made sense once but are now habit.

### SLACK / "QUICK ASK" DEFENSE SCRIPTS

**When: Someone's interrupting your work with urgent requests that aren't actually urgent.**

**Script 1 (Buy time)**
"I'm in the middle of something focused—can I circle back this afternoon?"

*Why it works:* Not refusing. Just protecting your focus time. Demonstrates you'll respond.

**Script 2 (Require context)**
"Can you drop this in a ticket/email so I can prioritize it properly with everything else?"

*Why it works:* Makes it formal enough that it won't slip, but on your timeline.

**Script 3 (Set an expectation)**
"I usually have a 2-hour response window on Slack—will that work for this?"

*Why it works:* Sets a clear boundary that people come to expect.

**Script 4 (If it's constant)**
"I'm getting a lot of these interruptions—can we find a better system for non-urgent asks?"

*Why it works:* Names the pattern. Suggests solving the systemic issue, not just refusing one request.

**Script 5 (Polite decline)**
"I'm not the right person for this—have you tried [person]?"

*Why it works:* Helpful redirection. Removes the expectation that you're the answer to everything.

### BOSS CONVERSATION SCRIPTS

**When: You need to push back to your manager about priorities or workload.**

**Script 1 (Alignment request)**
"I want to make sure I'm focused on what matters most to you. Can we align on priorities?"

*Why it works:* Positions it as wanting to do good work. Your boss gets to clarify what's actually important.

**Script 2 (The capacity question)**
"I have three major projects. I can't do all three equally well. What should take priority?"

*Why it works:* Forces a decision. Shows you're thinking about quality and priorities.

**Script 3 (Reality check)**
"I want to set realistic expectations—given everything on my plate, here's what I can realistically deliver."

*Why it works:* Honest. Prevents overpromising and underdelivering.

**Script 4 (Asking for help)**
"I'm running into a capacity issue. Do you want me to push back on new work, bring in a resource, or shift something?"

*Why it works:* You're identifying the problem and offering solutions.

**Script 5 (If they're overloading you)**
"I notice the load has increased significantly. Is that intentional, or should we recalibrate?"

*Why it works:* Gentle observation. Gives them a chance to course-correct without you sounding overwhelmed.

## CHAPTER 5: HOW TO SAY NO WITHOUT SOUNDING DEFENSIVE

Here's what kills most people's attempts at boundaries:

They say the right words, but with the wrong tone.

You can have the perfect script, and still come across as defensive, frustrated, or passive-aggressive.

The tone is everything.

### Tone Rules

**Rule 1: Stay calm and matter-of-fact**

Don't apologize endlessly. Don't sound frustrated. Don't sound like you're doing them a favor.

Just state it like it's a fact about the world.

"I don't have capacity for that" should sound like "It's raining outside."

Just information. Not emotional. Not argumentative.

**Rule 2: Don't over-explain**

This is where people lose power.

They say: "I'm at capacity" and then immediately add: "I mean, I have a lot going on, and I've been really busy with other projects, and I want to make sure I do good work..."

Stop.

The original sentence was enough.

Over-explaining makes you sound unsure. It invites pushback. "Well, actually, this is more important than..."

Say it once. Calmly. Then stop talking.

**Rule 3: Be honest, not strategic**

Don't lie about capacity to sound better. Don't pretend something's lower priority than it actually is.

People can sense when you're not being straight with them.

Honesty builds trust. Strategy builds resentment.

**Rule 4: Match your body language**

If you're saying no but looking apologetic, the message gets confused.

Sit up. Make eye contact. Speak at a normal pace.

Your body should say: *This is a reasonable boundary.*

Not: *I'm sorry for existing.*

### What Not to Do

**Don't sound annoyed.**
"I can't take this on right now" is fine.
"I'm already so busy, I can't take ANOTHER thing" sounds resentful.

**Don't make excuses.**
"I don't have capacity" is clear.
"I have a dentist appointment, and my kid has soccer, and I haven't eaten lunch..." is unclear and invites negotiation.

**Don't apologize for your boundaries.**
"I can't do that" is fine.
"I'm so sorry, I really wish I could, I feel terrible about this..." sounds like you don't actually have a boundary.

**Don't sound angry.**
Frustration comes across as aggression, even if you're not intending it.

If you're frustrated by constant asks, that's valid. But expressing it in the moment usually backfires.

Handle it later, separately.

### Practicing the Tone

Before you actually use a script, practice it out loud.

Seriously.

Say the line to yourself. Notice if you sound:
* Apologetic
* Annoyed
* Uncertain
* Defensive

Adjust until it sounds calm and clear.

You might feel weird doing this. Do it anyway.

The difference between sounding professional and sounding defensive is usually just practice.

## CHAPTER 6: WHEN YOU CAN'T SAY NO (AND WHAT TO DO INSTEAD)

Sometimes you can't say no.

Your boss directly tells you to do something. It's truly mandatory. The situation doesn't allow for negotiation.

Okay.

But you still have options.

The goal: protect your sanity and your other commitments without directly refusing.

### Strategy 1: Re-Scope

You can't refuse the work. But you can narrow what "done" looks like.

Instead of: "I can't do that."

Say: "I can do this scope by this date. Full scope would take longer."

Then deliver exactly what you said you'd deliver.

This is honest and it works.

### Strategy 2: Delay

You can't refuse now. But you can push it to a time when you actually have capacity.

"I can take this on, starting [date]. That gives me time to finish [current project]."

If they push back and want it sooner, that's when you use: "Moving it up means stopping work on X. What takes priority?"

Now they have to make the trade-off decision, not you.

### Strategy 3: Ask for Trade-Offs

"I can do this. That means [X] will be delayed/someone else needs to handle Y/I'll be staying late."

Make the cost visible.

Often, people realize they don't actually need it that badly once they see the real cost.

### Strategy 4: Document Everything

If you're being asked to do something unrealistic, put it in writing.

"Just to confirm: you want me to deliver X, Y, and Z by Friday, all while maintaining my current workload. Confirming this is the priority?"

In email. Clear. With dates.

If it goes sideways, you have a record.

If they push back later ("Why isn't this done?"), you can say: "This was the timeline we agreed to. Given everything, this is what's realistic."

### Strategy 5: Involve Your Manager

If you're stuck between competing demands, loop in your manager.

"I have conflicting priorities—X from you, Y from my boss. Which takes precedence?"

Don't force them to choose. Just ask for clarity.

Often that clarity makes the decision obvious.

### The Reality Check

If you're constantly in situations where you can't say no, that's a separate problem.

That usually means:

* Your workload is genuinely unsustainable
* Your manager doesn't understand capacity
* You need to have a bigger conversation

That conversation is outside the scope of this book.

But the answer isn't to keep accepting impossible workloads.

At some point, something has to give.

Either your hours, your quality, your health, or the role itself.

Pick the one you're willing to sacrifice.

Because you're already sacrificing something.

## CHAPTER 7: THE BOUNDARY FLYWHEEL

Here's the good news: once you say no the first time, it gets easier.

Actually—it gets better than easier. It gets self-reinforcing.

### Why the First No Is the Hardest

You've been saying yes for a long time. That's become the pattern.

When you break the pattern, it feels risky.

What if they react badly?

What if they resent you?

What if it damages the relationship?

Those are reasonable concerns.

And here's what actually happens:

They might be surprised. Maybe they're mildly disappointed.

And then—they move on.

They ask someone else. They adjust the deadline. They shrink the scope.

Life continues.

And you have more time.

The anticipatory anxiety is always worse than the actual reaction.

### How People Recalibrate

Once you've said no once, people's brains update.

They stop seeing you as someone who will say yes to anything.

They think: *Okay, I need to be more strategic with what I ask this person.*

That's exactly what you want.

Instead of throwing every project at you, they start prioritizing.

They pre-think about whether you're the right person.

They front-load the conversation: "I know you're busy, but..."

That's a good sign. That means they're thinking about your capacity.

### The Respect Follows Consistency

Here's the hidden principle:

People don't respect people who say yes to everything.

They respect people who are clear about their commitments.

If you say yes to something and then don't deliver, that erodes trust.

If you say no thoughtfully and then deliver on what you do commit to, that builds trust.

Over time, your no means something.

Your yes means something.

That's when people really start respecting your time.

### The Flywheel Effect

Once this happens, good things start compounding:

1. You say no to low-value work
2. You have more time for high-value work
3. Your work quality goes up
4. You get recognized for better outcomes
5. People prioritize what they ask you more carefully
6. You get fewer irrelevant requests
7. You say no less often because the asks are better
8. You have more energy and focus
9. You do even better work
10. The cycle repeats upward

This doesn't happen overnight.

But it does happen.

And it's usually faster than people expect.

## CHAPTER 8: BOUNDARIES WITH DIFFERENT PEOPLE

Not everyone requires the same approach.

Your boss, your peers, your team, your senior stakeholders—they all need slightly different frameworks.

### With Your Boss

Most people are terrified to push back to their boss.

But your boss probably wants honesty more than you think.

They want to know what's realistic. They want to avoid surprises. They want to know you'll tell them the truth.

What your boss doesn't want: for you to say yes and then miss deadlines, or burn out, or do mediocre work because you're overloaded.

**The principle: Frame it as wanting to do good work.**

Not: "I can't."

More like: "I want to make sure I deliver well on what matters most. Can we prioritize?"

Ask them to make the trade-off decision.

Most managers will actually be relieved.

It gives them permission to say: "Actually, focus on X. Y can wait."

### With Your Peers

This is usually easier because the power dynamic is more equal.

You can be more direct.

"I'm at capacity—can you ask someone else?"

"That doesn't fit my priorities right now."

"I can help with this, but not this week."

The key is reciprocity: you're not asking for something you wouldn't give back.

If you're the person who helps others with their capacity problems, you've built capital.

You can spend some of that capital saying no when you need to.

### With Your Team (If You Manage)

This is actually easier in some ways, harder in others.

Easier: You have authority to decide what's realistic.

Harder: You're responsible for the work getting done, so sometimes you can't just say no.

The framework:

* Be honest about capacity with your team
* Don't overload them
* Model saying no yourself (they'll see it's okay)
* Help them prioritize
* Protect them from unrealistic asks

When someone asks you for your team's time, you can say:

"I want to make sure they're focused on [priority]. Can [new ask] wait until [date]?"

You're saying no on their behalf.

That's part of your job.

### With Stakeholders

This is where it gets trickier because there's often a power imbalance and you don't have an ongoing relationship.

But the principle is the same: be clear about what's possible.

"I can deliver X by date Y. If you need more, we'd need to adjust timeline or scope."

Stakeholders usually respect clarity.

What they don't respect is overpromising and underdelivering.

## CHAPTER 9: BOUNDARY SETBACKS (What to Do When It Goes Wrong)

Sometimes you'll say no and it will go badly.

Not often. But sometimes.

Someone will get upset. A deadline will become a problem. A relationship will get tense.

What then?

### When Someone Gets Upset

First: stay calm. Don't get defensive.

Their reaction is their reaction. It's not your responsibility to manage it.

But you can clarify.

"I know this isn't ideal. My capacity is [real limitation]. I want to help solve this—what do you need?"

Often they'll propose a solution or realize they're overreacting.

Sometimes they won't. That's okay too.

You've stated a boundary. Whether they like it is up to them.

### When a Deadline Slips

If you push back on a deadline and then miss it anyway, that erodes credibility.

So: only push back on deadlines you actually can't meet.

If you say "I can deliver this by Friday," deliver it by Friday.

The whole point of boundaries is that they create trust.

Wavering on them destroys that.

### When a Relationship Gets Tense

Sometimes saying no to the wrong person or in the wrong way will create tension.

If that happens:

**Acknowledge it:** "I sensed some tension when I pushed back on that timeline. I want to make sure we're good."

**Clarify your intent:** "I'm not trying to be difficult. I genuinely can't deliver quality work if I'm overloaded."

**Offer to solve it:** "How can we handle this better next time?"

Usually, honesty and willingness to work on the relationship will get you back on track.

People are often more understanding than you expect.

### When You Realize You Were Wrong

Sometimes you'll say no and then realize you actually could have said yes.

Or you'll set a boundary that's too rigid.

It's okay to course-correct.

"I said I couldn't take that on, but I looked at my schedule and I actually can. Let me help."

This is fine. It shows flexibility and that you're genuinely trying to help.

Don't use this every time though. That erodes the boundary.

But occasionally? It builds goodwill.

## CHAPTER 10: BOUNDARIES UNDER PRESSURE

The times you most need boundaries are the times boundaries are hardest.

During crises, emergencies, launches, or tight deadlines—that's when people ask the most.

And that's when you're most tempted to say yes to everything.

### During Crises

A real crisis is an exception.

If the building's on fire, you don't worry about boundaries.

But most things people call "crises" aren't actually crises.

They're urgent, which is different.

Urgent = needs attention soon.

Crisis = existential threat to the business.

Don't treat every urgent thing like a crisis.

When someone says "this is urgent," ask: "Is this a genuine crisis, or is it just high-priority?"

You'll be surprised how often the answer is: "Well... high-priority."

Then you can handle it accordingly.

Real crises deserve boundary-breaking.

Artificial urgency doesn't.

### During Launches or Deadlines

These are when scope creep is worst.

"While we're focused on the launch, can we also..."

"Since you're already working on the deadline, could you..."

These requests sound reasonable. But they're exactly when you need boundaries most.

Say: "We're in launch mode. Let's focus on getting this done. Everything else is phase two."

Then actually defend that.

### When You're Tired

This is when boundaries matter most.

When you're exhausted, you have less patience, less focus, less ability to say no.

That's when you need to say no even more.

If you're tired, overloaded is the last thing you need.

Actually protect your time.

It's not selfish. It's the only way you'll perform well.

### When There's External Pressure

Sometimes the pressure comes from outside: a client, a deadline, a competitive threat.

Your instinct will be to say yes to everything in response.

But saying yes to everything usually means doing everything poorly.

The better move: get clear on priorities. Say no to things that don't support those priorities.

You'll actually be more helpful.

## CHAPTER 11: THE SYSTEMS THAT MAKE BOUNDARIES WORK

Boundaries aren't just about what you say.

They're about building systems that support them.

### The Weekly Priority Conversation

Every week, align with your manager (or yourself, if you're independent) on what matters.

Top 3-5 priorities.

Everything else is secondary.

When new requests come in, you have a standard answer:

"That sounds important. Where does it fall relative to [my top priorities]?"

Having a written list makes this easier.

You're not deciding in the moment. You're referencing a decision already made.

### The Meeting Audit

Go through your recurring meetings.

For each one: Do I need to be here?

If the answer is no, remove yourself.

Do this monthly.

You'll be surprised how many you can drop.

### The Slack Boundaries

Set a status message:

"I check Slack twice daily: 11 AM and 4 PM. For urgent items, use [phone/email/whatever]."

Set Do Not Disturb hours.

Don't get notifications outside of work hours.

These aren't rude. They're professional.

They create space for deep work.

### The Email Rules

Set expectations about response time.

"I respond to email within 24 hours, usually the next morning."

Then stick to it.

This removes the pressure to respond immediately.

People adjust their expectations.

### The Decision Framework

Before you say yes to anything, ask:

1. Does this align with my top priorities?
2. Do I have capacity for this?
3. What do I have to deprioritize?
4. Is this the best use of my time?

If the answer to 1 or 2 is no, the answer to the request should be no (or delayed).

Simple.

### Pick One Thing to Start With

You just have a little more time.

By the tenth time, it feels normal.

To focus on what matters.

To show up as your best self.

That's why this matters.

Not because you're being selfish.

## THE 10 MOST USEFUL ONE-LINERS

Keep these handy. Use them when you need them. Adapt them to your style.

**1. "I'm at capacity—what should I deprioritize?"**
Use when: Someone wants you to take on more work.
Why: Shifts the decision to them.

**2. "I don't have capacity for that right now."**
Use when: You need a simple, direct no.
Why: Clear and professional. No over-explaining.

**3. "I want to make sure I'm focused on the highest-impact work—where does this fall?"**
Use when: You need to push back but sound collaborative.
Why: Shows you care about priorities, not just refusing.

**4. "Given current priorities, I can deliver this by [date]."**
Use when: Someone wants it faster than realistic.
Why: States reality without being defensive.

**5. "That sounds like an expansion of the original scope—should we revisit the timeline?"**
Use when: Scope is creeping.
Why: Names what's happening without being accusatory.

**6. "I don't think I'm needed for this one—can you send notes if anything changes?"**
Use when: You're invited to a meeting that's not relevant.
Why: Helpful framing. Shows you care about outcomes.

**7. "I'm in the middle of something focused—can I circle back later today?"**
Use when: Someone's interrupting with a non-urgent request.
Why: Not refusing. Just protecting focus time. Respects their need, too.

**8. "I can take this on if we push back [X]. Does that work?"**
Use when: You want to say yes, but need to move something else.
Why: You're saying yes, with a real trade-off.

**9. "I want to make sure I'm aligned with your priorities—what should take precedence?"**
Use when: Talking to your boss about competing demands.
Why: Puts the decision on them, as it should be.

**10. "That timeline is tight—what's most critical for us to focus on?"**
Use when: You're at risk of being overloaded during a crunch.
Why: Forces prioritization instead of trying to do everything.
